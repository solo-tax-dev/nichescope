Best Etsy Research Tool: What to Actually Look For

If you search for Etsy research tools, you will find plenty of options promising to show you sales figures, revenue estimates, and demand scores. Those numbers look authoritative. The problem is that Etsy does not publish sales data. Whatever figure a tool shows you has been calculated by the tool itself, not provided by Etsy.

That matters more than most sellers realise. When you are deciding whether to spend weeks making a new product, the quality of the data underneath that decision determines whether the decision is sound.

This article covers what a genuinely useful Etsy research tool does, what the main options actually offer, and how to choose one that fits how you work.

What Makes an Etsy Research Tool Useful?

The core job of any research tool is to help you answer two questions before you commit time or money: is there real demand here, and how hard will it be to compete?

A tool is useful when it helps you answer those questions with evidence. It is less useful when it adds a layer of estimation that you cannot verify.

Data you can verify

Some information is genuinely visible on Etsy. Prices are public. Review counts are public. Whether a listing is marked as an ad is public. How many results appear for a search term is public. A tool that surfaces this information quickly and clearly gives you something you can check yourself.

Data you cannot verify

Sales volume is not published by Etsy. Revenue figures are not published. Conversion rates are not published. When a tool shows you a number for any of these, that number is an estimate built on assumptions the tool has made. The estimate might be directionally useful, or it might be significantly wrong. You have no way to tell from the outside.

This is not a criticism of any particular tool. It is just the reality of working with Etsy data. The sellers who get into trouble are those who treat estimated figures as if they were confirmed facts.

The Main Etsy Research Tools: What They Each Offer

eRank

eRank is one of the longest-established Etsy tools. It focuses primarily on keyword research and SEO, showing search volume estimates, competition scores, and trend data. It has a free tier with daily limits and a paid subscription for more queries. The search volume figures are estimates, not data Etsy publishes directly. eRank is widely used and reasonably well regarded for keyword work specifically.

EverBee

EverBee is a Chrome extension that overlays estimated revenue and sales data onto Etsy search results. The figures look precise, but they are calculated estimates rather than confirmed numbers. EverBee also has product analytics and shop analysis features. It has a free plan with limited functionality and paid tiers for fuller access.

Marmalead

Marmalead focuses on keyword and SEO optimisation for Etsy listings. It provides engagement scores, competition levels, and trend indicators. Like other tools, its underlying figures are estimates. It is subscription-based with no meaningful free tier.

Alura

Alura covers keyword research, listing optimisation, and estimated sales data. It takes a similar approach to EverBee in overlaying calculated figures onto Etsy pages. It has a free plan and paid tiers.

EtsyHunt

EtsyHunt is a product research tool with a large database of Etsy listings and keyword data. It shows estimated sales and revenue figures alongside on-page data. It has a free version with restrictions and paid plans for more access.

The One Thing All Estimated-Data Tools Share

Every tool above shows you numbers that Etsy has never confirmed. That does not make them worthless. Directional estimates can help you spot patterns, compare niches roughly, or identify keywords worth investigating further.

But there is a risk that comes with precise-looking numbers: they create a false sense of certainty. A seller who sees a listing estimated at a certain revenue figure may proceed with a product on that basis, without knowing how far off the estimate might be.

The more practical habit is to treat estimated figures as one weak signal among several, and to weight the things you can actually verify more heavily.

What Real, Verifiable Research Looks Like in Practice

When you open a search on Etsy, the page already contains a lot of usable information. Here is what experienced sellers tend to look at:

  • Price range across results: Where are most listings priced? Are there clusters at different price points? This tells you what buyers are used to paying and where there might be room to position differently.
  • Review counts on top listings: High review counts on the first page mean those sellers have been around a while and have built trust. It is harder to displace them on price or visibility alone.
  • Ad density: If most of the results on the first page are ads, organic ranking in that niche is difficult. If ads are sparse, there may be room to compete without heavy paid spend.
  • Listing quality and differentiation: Are all the listings very similar, or is there visible variety? Homogeneous results can signal a saturated niche where differentiation is hard.
  • Result count: A search returning hundreds of thousands of results is a different challenge from one returning tens of thousands. Neither is automatically good or bad, but the context matters.

None of this requires a paid subscription. It requires attention and a systematic way of recording what you find.

How to Use Multiple Tools Without Getting Confused

Most experienced sellers do not rely on a single tool. A sensible workflow might look like this:

  • Use keyword research tools (eRank, Marmalead) to generate a list of potentially relevant search terms, treating their volume estimates as rough signals rather than facts.
  • Check each term on Etsy itself to see what the actual search results look like: pricing, competition, ad density.
  • Use a tool that surfaces on-page data quickly to make that manual review faster and more consistent.
  • Make the product decision based on what you can verify, not what a tool has estimated.

This treats estimated data as a starting point for investigation, not a conclusion. It is slower than just reading a revenue figure and deciding, but it is more likely to lead you to a realistic assessment.

Pricing Research Is Just as Important as Niche Research

One area where on-page data is particularly valuable is pricing. If you are an existing seller reconsidering your prices, or a new seller trying to figure out where to enter a market, the price distribution across search results tells you a lot.

Are buyers in this niche used to paying a certain range? Are the highest-reviewed listings also the highest-priced, or the lowest? Is there a gap between what is available and what buyers might pay for something better made or better presented?

These questions can only be answered by looking at real prices on real listings. Estimated revenue figures do not help here at all. Systematic price research does.

The Honest Summary

There is no single best Etsy research tool for every seller, because different sellers need different things. If keyword optimisation is your main gap, a dedicated SEO tool makes sense. If you want a broad view of estimated market size, one of the estimated-data tools might give you a useful rough picture.

But if you are trying to make a product decision on solid ground, the most reliable data is the data that is genuinely on the page. Price it, count the reviews, look at the ads, read the competition honestly. That is less exciting than a revenue estimate, and considerably more trustworthy.
