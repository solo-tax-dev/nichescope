Etsy Competition Analysis: Four Things You Can Actually Measure

Before you spend time making a new product — or money on materials and photography — it makes sense to look at who you are up against on Etsy. The problem is that most of the tools designed to help with this hand you a set of numbers that Etsy has never actually published: estimated monthly sales, estimated revenue, estimated conversion rates.

Those estimates are not worthless, but they are estimates. Etsy does not share sales data with third-party tools, so any figure labelled 'monthly sales' is a model output, not a verified fact. That matters when you are basing a business decision on it.

This guide takes a different approach. It focuses on four signals that are genuinely on the page — things you can see with your own eyes and verify in seconds. Used together, they give you a realistic picture of how competitive a niche is and whether there is room for you in it.

Why Most Etsy Competition Analysis Goes Wrong

Sellers usually start in one of two places: they search a keyword on Etsy and feel immediately overwhelmed by the number of results, or they run a tool that shows impressive-looking revenue figures and either feel encouraged or discouraged based on those numbers.

Both reactions can lead you astray. A high listing count does not automatically mean a niche is closed to new sellers. And a revenue estimate built on modelled assumptions can point you toward a product that does not actually sell, or away from one that does.

The more useful habit is to slow down and read what is actually in front of you. Etsy search results contain a surprising amount of signal if you know what to look for.

The Four Signals That Are Actually on the Page

1. Listing Count

The total number of results Etsy shows for a search term is the most visible competition metric — and the most misread one. A niche with tens of thousands of listings is not automatically too crowded. A niche with a few hundred listings is not automatically easy to enter.

What listing count really tells you is the size of the supply side. Pair it with everything else below and it becomes meaningful. On its own, it is just a number.

Practical tip: look at the spread of listings across pages. If the first page is dominated by a handful of shops with thousands of sales each and everything after page two looks thin and poorly made, the niche has a concentration problem — a few established sellers are absorbing most of the demand. If quality and engagement are more evenly spread, there is more room to compete on merit.

2. Review Counts

Reviews are the closest thing Etsy gives you to a verified engagement signal. You cannot see sales directly, but a listing with two thousand reviews has clearly sold a meaningful number of items. A listing with three reviews launched last month is a very different beast.

When you scan the first page of results for a keyword, notice the distribution of review counts. Ask yourself:

  • Are most top results sitting on hundreds or thousands of reviews, or are there well-reviewed and lightly-reviewed listings mixed together?
  • Are there any listings on the first page with relatively few reviews? That suggests Etsy is willing to surface newer or smaller sellers in this niche.
  • How old are the most-reviewed listings? A shop that built three thousand reviews over eight years is different from one that did it in eighteen months.

Review counts are not sales figures, but they are real. Every review represents at least one completed transaction and a buyer who chose to leave feedback. That makes them a dependable anchor when everything else is estimated.

3. Ad Density

Look at the first page of results and count how many listings are marked as ads. Etsy labels promoted listings clearly, so this is straightforward to do.

A high proportion of ads on page one tells you a few things:

  • Sellers in this niche are willing to pay to be seen, which generally means the economics support it — products have enough margin or volume to justify ad spend.
  • Organic ranking is harder to achieve when ads take up a large share of visible space.
  • New sellers face a steeper climb to get organic visibility, because the prime real estate is being bought rather than earned through listing quality alone.

On the flip side, a niche where page one is mostly organic results suggests that ranking well is achievable without a significant advertising budget. That is useful information whether you are just starting out or trying to decide where to focus your effort.

Ad density is easy to check manually, but it is easy to lose track of across multiple searches. Having a consistent way to record it — even a simple spreadsheet — helps when you are comparing several niches at once.

4. Price Spread

Scan the price points across the first two pages of results and note the range. You are looking for a few things:

  • The floor: what is the lowest price a legitimate listing is charging? This is often set by mass-produced or print-on-demand sellers who can undercut handmade sellers on price. If the floor is very low, competing on price alone is rarely a winning strategy.
  • The ceiling: how high do prices go, and are those higher-priced listings getting reviews? A high price ceiling with reviews attached tells you buyers in this niche are willing to spend more for the right product.
  • The cluster: where do most listings sit? If the majority are bunched around a narrow price band, that is effectively the market rate. If prices are spread widely, there may be room to position at a different point.

Price spread also helps you reality-check your own cost structure. If your materials and time mean you need to charge more than most listings on the first page, you need a clear differentiator — better photos, a stronger brand, a more specific niche — to justify that to buyers.

How to Use These Four Signals Together

None of these signals is definitive on its own. The real insight comes from reading them as a set.

A niche with a high listing count, review counts heavily concentrated in a few shops, high ad density on page one, and a tight price cluster near the bottom of what you could sustainably charge is a hard niche to enter. Not impossible, but hard — and you should go in with clear eyes.

A niche with a moderate listing count, reviews spread across many different sellers, lower ad density, and a wide price spread — including well-reviewed listings at higher prices — is a more open one. There is demand, buyers are not all going to the same two or three shops, and there appears to be room to earn visibility organically.

Most niches fall somewhere between those two examples, and reading the signals helps you understand where on that spectrum you are looking.

A Practical Workflow for Checking a Niche

Here is a straightforward process you can run in under fifteen minutes per niche:

  • Search your target keyword on Etsy in a fresh browser tab (not logged in, to reduce personalisation).
  • Note the total listing count Etsy reports.
  • Count how many of the first-page results are ads.
  • Scroll through pages one and two, noting the range of review counts and where most listings cluster.
  • Note the lowest, highest, and most common price points.
  • Repeat for two or three related keyword variations — the results often differ more than you would expect.

A Note on Tools That Show Sales Estimates

Tools like eRank, EverBee, and Alura are widely used and have genuine value — particularly for keyword research and tracking your own shop performance. When they show estimated sales or revenue figures, those numbers are based on modelling: the tool makes assumptions about conversion rates and traffic to produce an estimate.

That is not dishonest — the tools are transparent that these are estimates. But it does mean you should treat those figures as directional rather than factual. Two different tools often produce meaningfully different estimates for the same listing, which illustrates the uncertainty involved.

The four signals covered in this article are not estimates. They are observable facts. Using them alongside any other research you do gives you a more grounded basis for your decisions.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if an Etsy niche is too competitive?

There is no universal threshold, but the combination of high ad density on page one, review counts heavily concentrated in a handful of long-established shops, and a price floor you cannot sustainably meet is a reasonable warning sign. Any one of those factors alone is not decisive — it is the combination that matters.

Can I do Etsy competition analysis without paying for a tool?

Yes. Everything in this article — listing count, review counts, ad density, price spread — is visible on Etsy search results pages without any paid tool. NicheScope also offers a free extension that organises this data as you browse, with no account or subscription required.

Are estimated sales figures from Etsy tools reliable?

They are estimates based on modelling assumptions, not data Etsy publishes. They can give you a rough directional sense of demand, but different tools often produce different estimates for the same listing. Treat them as one input, not as a verified fact.

How many keywords should I check before deciding on a product?

Check the main keyword buyers would use to find your product, plus two or three natural variations. Results can differ significantly between phrasings — a niche that looks very competitive under one search term may look more accessible under a slightly different one. Checking several gives you a more complete picture.
