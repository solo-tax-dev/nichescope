Etsy Competition Checker: How to Read a Niche Before You Commit

Before you spend money on materials, photography, or listing fees, it makes sense to look at the market you are walking into. That means checking who is already selling, what they charge, how established they are, and how hard Etsy is pushing paid ads in that space. None of that requires guesswork — it is all sitting on the search results page if you know what to look for.

This guide walks through how to use an Etsy competition checker properly, what signals actually matter, and why the numbers some tools show you may be less solid than they appear.

What Does an Etsy Competition Checker Actually Do?

The phrase gets used loosely, so it is worth being precise. A competition checker for Etsy should help you answer a few concrete questions: How many sellers are in this space? What are they charging? How long have the top listings been around? Are ads crowding out organic results?

Some tools go further and try to estimate how many sales a listing or shop has made, or project monthly revenue. Those figures are genuinely useful if they are accurate — but Etsy does not publish sales data. Any tool showing you an estimated sales count is working from a model, not from Etsy itself. That is not a criticism of those tools; it is just something worth understanding when you are making a business decision based on the numbers.

Tools like EverBee, eRank, and Alura all offer estimated sales or revenue figures. They are popular and many sellers find them helpful. The limitation is that the estimates are inferred, not reported — so two tools can show meaningfully different numbers for the same listing.

A different approach is to check only what is actually visible on the page: prices, review counts, listing age where shown, and how many of the top results are paid ads. That data is exact, because it comes straight from Etsy.

The Signals Worth Checking in Any Niche

Review Count Distribution

Look at the review counts across the first page or two of results. If the top ten listings each have several thousand reviews and have been running for years, you are looking at an entrenched market. That does not mean you cannot enter it, but it does mean you need a clear reason why a buyer would choose you over someone with a long track record.

If review counts are spread out — some listings with hundreds, some with very few — that suggests the niche is still relatively open. New listings can get traction without being buried by established players.

This is not about finding a magic threshold. It is about reading the shape of the competition before you invest time and money.

Price Range and Clustering

What is the going rate for this product? Look at whether prices are tightly clustered or spread across a wide range. A tight cluster — say, most listings sitting within a narrow band — usually means buyers have a strong price expectation and listings that deviate get ignored. A wider spread often means there is room to position at different quality or value levels.

Pay attention to outliers too. If a handful of listings are priced significantly higher than the rest and they have strong review counts, that tells you buyers will pay more for the right product. If the high-priced listings have few reviews, that might just mean people tried and it did not work.

Ad Density

Etsy labels paid listings, so you can count them directly. If a high proportion of the results on page one are ads, that tells you organic visibility in this niche is competitive enough that sellers are paying to stay visible. That is a cost of doing business you need to factor in if you want similar placement.

A niche with low ad density and decent review counts across multiple listings can be a sign of a space where organic search is still doing the work — which is generally better for a new seller without an ad budget.

Listing Age and Freshness

Etsy does not always make listing age obvious, but review timestamps give you a proxy. If the top listings have reviews going back five or more years, those sellers have had a long time to accumulate social proof. That compounds — older listings with many reviews tend to rank better, which brings more sales, which brings more reviews.

Newer listings with strong recent review activity suggest the niche is still generating demand and that the algorithm is surfacing newer sellers. That is the environment you want to enter if you are starting from scratch.

A Practical Workflow for Checking Competition

Here is a straightforward approach that does not require any paid subscription:

  • Search your target keyword on Etsy and note the total result count.
  • Scan the first two pages and record the price range, the ad placements, and the review counts of the top ten organic listings.
  • Look at two or three of the strongest listings and check their review history to gauge how long they have been active.
  • Run the same search with two or three variant keywords — singular vs plural, with location, with style descriptor — to see if the competitive picture changes.
  • Make a simple judgement: is the top of this market locked up by established shops, or is there visible space for a newer listing to compete?

Common Mistakes When Reading Etsy Competition

Treating Result Count as the Main Signal

A search returning a large number of results does not necessarily mean the niche is overcrowded. It means a lot of listings use that keyword. What matters more is the quality and establishment level of the top results — those are the listings you are actually competing with for page-one visibility.

Ignoring Pricing Context

Checking whether you can price profitably in a niche is as important as checking whether you can rank in it. If the market price for a product is below what you need to charge to cover your costs and make a return, the demand level is irrelevant. Look at prices before you look at anything else.

Assuming Estimated Sales Numbers Are Precise

As noted above, sales estimates from third-party tools are models, not measurements. They can point you in a useful direction, but making a major business decision based on a specific estimated revenue figure is risky. Where possible, cross-reference with signals you can verify directly — review counts, pricing, ad activity.

Checking Only One Keyword

Buyers do not all search the same way. A niche that looks saturated under one phrase might have a much more open competitive picture under a related term. Always check a handful of variations before concluding a space is or is not worth entering.

Before You Make the Product: A Checklist

  • Is the price range on page one profitable for you after fees and materials?
  • Are the top organic listings dominated by a small number of very established shops, or distributed across multiple sellers?
  • What proportion of page-one results are paid ads?
  • Are there listings with strong reviews that are relatively recent, suggesting ongoing demand?
  • Have you checked at least three keyword variants to get a fuller picture?

If you can answer these questions with actual data — not assumptions — you are in a much stronger position than most sellers who simply list something and hope for the best.
