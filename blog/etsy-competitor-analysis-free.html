Etsy Competitor Analysis Free: How to Size Up the Competition Without Paying for Guesses

If you sell on Etsy — or you're thinking about it — you've probably wondered how much competition you're really up against in a given niche. You may have already tried one of the popular research tools, only to find yourself staring at revenue estimates and sales figures that nobody can actually verify. This guide is about doing that analysis properly, using only data that's genuinely there in front of you, and doing it without spending a penny.

Why Most Free Etsy Research Tools Aren't Quite Free

Several well-known Etsy research tools — eRank, EverBee, Alura and others — offer some form of free tier. That's genuinely useful up to a point. But the headline features most sellers want, things like estimated monthly sales or estimated revenue per listing, are usually locked behind a paid plan. When you do get access, those numbers are modelled estimates, not figures Etsy publishes. The tools are upfront about this in their own documentation if you read it carefully.

That's not a criticism of those tools. Estimating is hard, and there's clearly demand for it. The problem is when sellers treat estimates as facts and make stock, pricing, or product decisions based on them. A number that looks precise — say, 47 sales last month — carries a lot of psychological weight even when it's a model output rather than a verified figure.

Genuinely free competitor research means using data you can actually see, not data someone has inferred for you.

What Data Is Actually on the Page

Etsy doesn't publish sales figures publicly. What it does show, on every listing and every shop page, is a surprisingly useful set of signals:

  • Review count — not the same as sales, but a real, verifiable number that reflects accumulated buyer activity over time.
  • Price — exactly what it says. Useful for understanding the range a niche supports.
  • Number of listings in a shop — tells you something about how established a seller is and how broadly they're covering a niche.
  • Ad density in search results — how many of the listings on a results page are paid placements. A useful proxy for how commercially competitive a niche is.
  • Listing age signals — some listings show when they were first published or last updated, which helps you gauge how long it takes to build traction.

None of this is secret. It's all visible to anyone who opens Etsy in a browser. The inconvenience is that gathering it manually, across dozens of listings, takes a long time. That's where a browser extension becomes genuinely useful — not to invent new data, but to surface what's already there more quickly.

How to Actually Do Etsy Competitor Analysis for Free

Step 1: Search the niche as a buyer would

Start with the search term a real customer would use, not a keyword-tool suggestion. Type it into Etsy as if you were shopping. Look at the first two pages of results. Notice immediately: how many of those listings are ads? If most of the above-the-fold results are paid placements, that tells you sellers in this niche are actively spending to stay visible — which suggests organic ranking is competitive.

Step 2: Look at review counts across the top results

Click into the top ten or fifteen listings. Don't worry about estimated sales. Look at the review count. A listing with several hundred reviews has been around long enough, and sold enough, to accumulate them. That's a real signal of durable demand. A cluster of listings all sitting at very low review counts might mean the niche is newer or less proven — useful to know either way.

Compare review counts across price points. Sometimes you'll find that the listings with the most reviews aren't the cheapest ones — that's a signal the market isn't purely price-driven, and there's room for differentiation on quality or presentation.

Step 3: Check shop depth and specialisation

Visit the shops behind the top listings. How many listings does each shop have? A shop with hundreds of listings in a single niche is a very different competitive proposition to one with a handful. Also notice whether the shop is tightly focused on one product type or spread across many. Focused shops that dominate a niche tend to do so because buyers find them more credible for that specific thing.

Step 4: Map the price range honestly

Note the lowest, highest, and most common prices in the results you're looking at. You don't need a tool for this — a quick scan and a few notes will do. What you're looking for is where the cluster sits. If most listings are priced between two points, and outliers at either end have notably fewer reviews, that price band is probably where the market has settled. Pricing outside it isn't impossible, but you'll want a clear reason why.

Step 5: Check a few shop announcement sections and listing descriptions

This sounds low-tech, and it is. But reading what established sellers say about their products, their turnaround times, and their USPs tells you something no algorithm can: how sellers in this niche are positioning themselves, and where the gaps might be.

What On-Page Data Can and Can't Tell You

Being honest about the limits of this approach matters. On-page data tells you what the competitive landscape looks like right now, from the outside. It doesn't tell you:

  • How many units a specific listing has sold
  • What a seller's profit margin is
  • Whether a shop's traffic is rising or falling
  • What keywords a listing is ranking for organically

Some of those things can be estimated — and for some sellers, an imperfect estimate is still useful. But it's worth being clear in your own thinking about which decisions you're basing on solid ground and which on inference. For pricing and competitive positioning, the on-page data is solid. For demand forecasting, it's a starting point at best.

Putting It Together Before You Commit

Whether you're validating a product idea or reviewing an existing listing's competitive position, the same basic question applies: does the evidence on the page suggest this niche has real, sustained demand, and is there a position in it that isn't already saturated?

Review counts across multiple listings suggest sustained demand. A spread of price points with healthy review counts at multiple levels suggests the market isn't bottlenecked at the cheapest option. Low ad density suggests it's not yet so competitive that organic visibility requires significant ad spend.

None of this requires a subscription, an account, or trust in a black-box model. It requires a methodical look at what's already visible.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I really do Etsy competitor analysis without paying for a tool?

Yes. Etsy shows prices, review counts, listing counts, and ad placements publicly. Those are real, verifiable signals that tell you a great deal about competitive intensity and market health. Paid tools tend to add estimated sales and revenue figures on top — useful to some sellers, but not the only way to do meaningful research.

Are Etsy sales estimates from tools like EverBee or eRank accurate?

Those tools are transparent that their figures are estimates, not data Etsy publishes. How accurate they are in practice varies, and neither Etsy nor the tools themselves publish verified accuracy comparisons. Treat estimated figures as one signal among many rather than as ground truth.

What's the difference between review count and sales volume on Etsy?

Review count is a real, public number. Sales volume is not published by Etsy and cannot be directly observed. Only a fraction of buyers leave reviews, so review count significantly understates total sales — but it's consistent and verifiable, which makes it a more reliable comparative signal than a modelled sales estimate.

What should I look at in Etsy search results to judge competition quickly?

Ad density (how many of the top results are sponsored listings), review counts on the top organic results, the price range of those results, and the depth of the shops behind them. Together these give you a reasonable read on how established and commercially active a niche is, without needing any data beyond what Etsy shows you.
