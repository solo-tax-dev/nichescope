EtsyHunt for Etsy Product Research: An Honest Overview

If you've been looking for ways to research Etsy products before committing time and money to a new idea, you've probably come across EtsyHunt. It's one of several tools in this space, and it gets mentioned a lot in Etsy seller communities. This article walks through what EtsyHunt actually does, where its approach has limitations, and what else you might use alongside it.

What EtsyHunt Is

EtsyHunt is a browser-based research tool aimed at Etsy sellers. It lets you search for products and keywords, and it surfaces data intended to help you judge whether a niche is worth entering. The free tier gives you a limited number of daily searches; paid plans unlock more.

Its main features include a product database, a keyword tool, a shop analyser, and a browser extension. On the surface, that's a solid-looking toolkit for product research.

The Estimated Data Question

Here's the thing worth understanding before you rely on any Etsy research tool: Etsy does not publish sales data. The platform doesn't share how many units a listing has sold, what revenue a shop generates, or how a listing ranks internally. That information is private.

Tools like EtsyHunt — along with others such as eRank, EverBee, and Alura — fill that gap with estimated figures. They use their own models and whatever signals they can gather to produce numbers that look like sales counts or revenue totals. Those estimates can be useful as rough directional signals, but they are not sourced from Etsy directly. The accuracy varies, and there's no way to verify them against ground truth because Etsy doesn't publish the real numbers.

This isn't a criticism unique to EtsyHunt — it applies across the category. The honest framing is: these are educated guesses, not confirmed figures. Treat them as one input, not as fact.

What EtsyHunt's Free Plan Actually Gives You

The free plan restricts how many product searches and keyword lookups you can do per day. For occasional research it may be enough to get a feel for a niche. For ongoing research across multiple product ideas, you'll hit the limits fairly quickly.

The browser extension adds some overlay data while you browse Etsy search results. Again, some of that data is estimated rather than sourced directly from Etsy's pages.

Where EtsyHunt Works Well

Despite the caveats above, EtsyHunt can be useful in some specific situations:

  • Early-stage idea generation: Browsing a wide product database can surface categories you hadn't thought of. Even rough signals can prompt useful questions.
  • Keyword brainstorming: Seeing what search terms exist around a product type helps you think about how buyers describe what they want.
  • Competitive landscape overview: Looking at which shops appear repeatedly in a niche gives you a sense of who the established players are.

The key is using any estimated data as a starting point for further investigation, not as a conclusion in itself.

What to Verify With Real On-Page Data

Once a tool like EtsyHunt has pointed you toward a niche, the next step is grounding your research in what's actually visible on Etsy. This is where estimated figures can lead you astray if taken at face value.

Real, verifiable signals you can check directly on Etsy include:

  • Review counts: A listing with a large number of reviews has clearly sold a meaningful volume over time. You don't need an estimate — the number is right there.
  • Prices: What are the actual price points in the search results? What's the range from lowest to highest? Where do the most-reviewed listings sit on that range?
  • Ad density: How many of the top results are paid ads versus organic listings? A high proportion of ads can indicate either strong commercial interest or thin organic competition — context matters.
  • Listing age and review recency: A shop with many reviews accumulated over several years tells a different story than one that gathered the same number more recently.

None of these require a paid subscription or a model built on guesses. They're on the page. The challenge is reading them efficiently across a full search results page.

A Practical Research Approach

Rather than relying on any single tool, most experienced Etsy sellers use a layered approach:

Step 1: Generate ideas broadly

Use keyword tools and product databases — EtsyHunt included — to surface niches you might not have considered. Don't treat the estimated numbers as gospel at this stage; you're looking for directions to investigate, not conclusions.

Step 2: Search Etsy directly

Run the actual search on Etsy. Look at what appears. How many total results are there? What do the top listings look like? Are most of the top results ads or organic?

Step 3: Read the on-page data carefully

Look at review counts on the top listings. Check prices across the results. Look at whether the best-reviewed listings are priced at the high or low end of the range. This is the data that's actually real.

Step 4: Assess entry difficulty honestly

A niche where the top ten results all have thousands of reviews is genuinely hard to enter. One where several top listings have modest review counts may have more room. You don't need estimated sales figures to see this — you need to read the page.

Step 5: Price based on market reality

Once you understand the competitive landscape, price relative to what's actually on the market — not relative to estimated revenue figures from a third-party model.

Pricing Research Specifically

EtsyHunt and similar tools sometimes show price data alongside their estimated sales figures. The prices themselves are real (they come from listings), but be careful about conclusions that mix real prices with estimated volumes. If a tool tells you a £12 listing is outselling a £18 listing by a specific ratio, that ratio is modelled, not measured.

For pricing research, looking directly at the price distribution across a search results page — which listings sit at which price points and how many reviews they've accumulated — gives you a more reliable picture than any estimated revenue ranking.

The Bottom Line on EtsyHunt

EtsyHunt is a legitimate tool used by a large number of Etsy sellers. Its keyword brainstorming features and product database can be genuinely useful, especially for idea generation. Like all tools in this category, its estimated sales and revenue figures are models, not facts from Etsy — and that's worth keeping in mind as you weight the data it gives you.

The most reliable Etsy research combines broad exploration (where tools like EtsyHunt help) with careful reading of what's actually visible on the Etsy platform. Neither step replaces the other.

FAQ: EtsyHunt and Etsy Product Research

Is EtsyHunt free to use?

EtsyHunt has a free tier that allows a limited number of searches per day. If you need to research multiple niches regularly, you're likely to hit those limits. Paid plans remove or increase those limits. It's worth testing the free version first to see whether the daily allowance fits your workflow.

How accurate are EtsyHunt's sales estimates?

EtsyHunt, like other Etsy research tools, produces estimated sales figures using its own modelling — Etsy does not share actual sales data with third parties. The estimates can give a rough directional sense of relative popularity, but they cannot be verified against real figures. Treat them as approximations rather than accurate counts.

What's the difference between EtsyHunt and tools like eRank or EverBee?

All three offer keyword research, product research, and estimated sales or revenue data. Their approaches and interfaces differ, and their free tiers have different limits. The underlying limitation is the same across all of them: the sales estimates are modelled, not sourced from Etsy. Each tool has different strengths in how it presents keyword data and how broad its product database is.

Can I do Etsy product research without relying on estimated figures?

Yes. Review counts, prices, and ad density are all visible directly on Etsy search results pages and represent real data. Combining keyword brainstorming tools with careful reading of actual search results pages gives you a research process grounded in what's verifiably true. Tools that focus on on-page data — rather than estimates — can make that reading process faster and more systematic.
