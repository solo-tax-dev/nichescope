EverBee Alternative Free: What to Use Instead for Etsy Research

If you have been looking for a free EverBee alternative, you have probably already noticed that most Etsy research tools follow the same pattern: sign up, connect your shop, and pay a monthly fee for access to estimated sales figures. EverBee is one of the better-known tools in that category. It is not a bad product, but it is not free, and the core numbers it shows — estimated sales, estimated revenue — are figures Etsy has never published publicly.

That is worth understanding before you make any sourcing or pricing decision based on what a tool tells you a competitor is earning.

Why Estimated Sales Numbers Are Tricky

Etsy does not share individual shop or listing sales data with third parties. What tools like EverBee, Alura, and eRank show as 'sales estimates' are reverse-engineered figures, usually built by tracking review count velocity and making assumptions from there. The methodology is reasonable in theory, but reviews are optional, review rates vary wildly by niche, and seasonal spikes or promotional periods can throw estimates off considerably.

None of that makes these tools dishonest. They are upfront that these are estimates. The problem is that once you see a number in a dashboard, it is psychologically hard to treat it as an approximation. You might decide to enter a niche, price a product, or pass on an idea based on a figure that could be substantially off in either direction.

If the number could be wrong, it is worth asking: what data can you actually verify yourself?

What Is Actually Visible on an Etsy Search Page

When you run a search on Etsy, the results page shows you quite a lot of useful, verifiable information:

  • Prices — exactly what each seller is charging for that item
  • Review counts — the number of reviews a listing has received (a rough but real proxy for historical demand)
  • Ad density — how many of the visible results are paid ads versus organic listings
  • Listing age signals — when a listing was created or last updated, visible on the listing page itself

None of these require estimation. They are right there on the page. And collectively, they can tell you a lot: whether a niche is dominated by a handful of established sellers, whether the price range is tight or spread out, whether sellers are spending heavily on ads to compete, and whether review counts suggest genuine traction or thin demand.

How to Use On-Page Data for Etsy Research

Validating a New Product Idea

Before you invest time or money making a product, you want to know three things: Is anyone buying this? Is the market crowded? And can you price competitively and still make a margin?

Review counts help with the first question. If the top results for your target keyword have hundreds or thousands of reviews, there is clearly demand. If every listing has fewer than ten reviews, the niche may be too thin, too new, or too competitive at the top for newer sellers to gain traction.

Ad density helps with the second. A search page where most results are ads tells you sellers are paying to be seen — which usually means organic ranking is hard. A page with few ads may mean the niche is less contested, or that the margins do not support ad spend.

Price spread helps with the third. If prices cluster tightly, the market has settled on a going rate. If they are scattered, there may be room to position differently — either on quality or on value.

Researching Existing Competition

If you already sell on Etsy and want to understand where you sit relative to competitors, the same data points apply. Looking at how your prices compare to what ranks well, whether high-review listings are priced above or below you, and how much of the visible page is paid placements can help you make smarter decisions about pricing and whether ad spend makes sense for your listings.

This kind of research does not need estimated revenue. It needs honest benchmarks — and those are available directly from the page.

The Case for Starting With Real Data

There is a practical argument for building your research habit around verifiable numbers, even if you later supplement with estimated data from paid tools. When your conclusions are based on things you can confirm yourself — prices you can see, review counts you can count, ads you can spot — you can defend those conclusions and adjust your thinking as the market changes.

When your conclusions rest on estimates, you are always one methodology change away from having the floor shift under you.

Starting with what is real does not mean ignoring estimates entirely. It means knowing what you know versus what you are inferring.
