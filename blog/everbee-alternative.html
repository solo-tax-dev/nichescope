EverBee Alternative: Why Verified Data Beats Revenue Estimates

If you have spent any time researching Etsy tools, you have probably encountered EverBee. It sits in a crowded category alongside eRank, Alura, and a handful of others, all promising to show you how much money a listing or shop is making. That sounds enormously useful. The problem is that Etsy does not publish sales revenue data to third parties. So where do those figures come from?

Where Do Estimated Sales Numbers Actually Come From?

Tools like EverBee use publicly visible review counts and apply multipliers — informed assumptions about how many sales result in a review — to produce an estimated sales or revenue figure. The methodology varies between tools, and some are more transparent about it than others. None of them are pulling a number from Etsy's database, because Etsy does not share that data.

That is not a criticism of these tools specifically. Estimation is a legitimate research technique. But there is a meaningful difference between an estimate built on assumptions and a number you can verify yourself. When you are deciding whether to spend weeks creating a product line, that difference matters.

The review-to-sale ratio Etsy buyers leave reviews at varies by category, price point, buyer demographics, and whether a seller follows up to request a review. A tool using a single multiplier across all categories will be more accurate in some niches than others. You have no way of knowing which situation you are in.

What You Can Actually Verify on an Etsy Search Page

Before you pay for any tool, it is worth being clear about what information Etsy does make publicly available, because it is genuinely useful:

  • Review counts per listing — a rough proxy for relative popularity, not an exact sales figure
  • Prices — what competitors are charging right now
  • Whether a listing is an ad or organic result — ad density tells you how commercially contested a niche is
  • Total result count — how many listings compete for a given search term
  • Listing age and recency signals — visible on some listings, indicating how long a product has been live

None of this gives you an exact revenue figure. But together it paints a reliable picture of the competitive landscape, because every data point is something you can see with your own eyes on the page.

The Practical Problem With Estimated Revenue Figures

Here is a pattern that comes up regularly among Etsy sellers: a tool shows a listing earning an impressive estimated monthly revenue, the seller enters the niche, and the reality turns out to be considerably more modest. Was the tool wrong? Possibly. Or the listing had a strong seasonal spike, or the review multiplier was off for that category, or the listing had since declined. Estimates are a snapshot built on assumptions, and assumptions have error margins.

When you make a product decision based on an overestimated revenue figure, you might invest time, materials, and listing fees into a niche that looked more profitable than it is. When you make the same decision based on verified signals — price range, review volume relative to competitors, ad density — you are working from ground truth even if the picture is less precise.

This is not about one tool being dishonest. It is about understanding what kind of confidence you can place in different types of information.

How to Actually Research a Niche Without Revenue Estimates

You do not need estimated revenue figures to make a sound product decision. Here is a practical framework using only verifiable data:

1. Check the price range

Look at what the top organic listings are charging. If prices cluster tightly, there is pricing pressure — buyers have trained expectations. If there is a wide spread, there may be room to position at a premium. This is real data you can act on.

2. Look at review counts, relatively not absolutely

Do not try to convert reviews into sales. Instead, compare listings to each other. If the top five results each have thousands of reviews and you are starting from zero, you face a significant authority gap. If most listings in the results have low review counts, the niche may be newer or less saturated.

3. Measure ad density

Count how many of the first page results are ads. A high proportion of ads means sellers are paying to compete — which suggests organic ranking is difficult and margins may be under pressure. A low proportion can indicate either low commercial intent or an accessible organic opportunity.

4. Look at total result count

A search term returning a very large number of results is not automatically bad, but it does mean you need a way to stand out. A smaller result count with decent review counts on top listings can indicate a niche with genuine demand but manageable competition.

5. Repeat across several related search terms

Buyers rarely search in exactly one way. Look at variations of your target term. Consistent patterns across several searches are more reliable than a single data point.

This process takes longer without a tool aggregating it for you, but every number in your analysis will be one you verified yourself.
