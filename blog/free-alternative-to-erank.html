Free Alternative to eRank: What It Shows, What It Estimates, and a Cleaner Starting Point

If you have spent any time researching Etsy tools, you have probably come across eRank. It is one of the most widely used platforms for Etsy keyword research and listing analysis, and it has a free tier that a lot of sellers start with. But there is a question worth asking before you build your product research around any tool: what is the data actually based on?

This article breaks down what eRank genuinely shows you, where it is making educated guesses, and why a simpler approach — one that only uses data visible on the Etsy page itself — might be a better starting point, especially when you are validating an idea before committing time or money.

What eRank Actually Shows You

eRank pulls in data from a few different places. Some of it is solid. Keyword search volume figures, for example, come from the Etsy API, which eRank has licensed access to. That means when eRank tells you how many times a phrase was searched on Etsy in a given month, that number has a real source behind it — it is not invented.

eRank also surfaces listing-level information: tags, titles, categories, shipping details. A lot of this is scraped or pulled directly from public Etsy pages, so it reflects what is actually there.

Where things get murkier is when you move into sales and revenue estimates.

The Estimation Problem

Etsy does not publish sales figures. They never have. A shop might display a total review count, and you can sometimes see a rough sold count on individual listings, but Etsy does not give any third-party tool a direct feed of transaction data.

So when eRank — or any other Etsy tool — shows you an estimated monthly revenue or an estimated number of sales for a shop or listing, that figure is a model. It is built from review counts, listing age, pricing, category averages, and other signals. The methodology is reasonable, and eRank is transparent that these are estimates. But they are still estimates, and in competitive or niche markets they can be significantly off.

This is not a criticism unique to eRank. EverBee, Alura, and every other tool in this space faces the same constraint: Etsy does not share sales data, so anyone showing you sales numbers is showing you a calculated guess, not a fact. Some tools are more upfront about this than others.

Why Estimated Sales Numbers Can Lead You Astray

The practical problem is not that estimates exist — it is that they can feel authoritative when they are displayed in a clean dashboard with decimal places and trend charts. A seller validating a new product idea might see an estimated monthly revenue figure and treat it as ground truth when deciding whether to invest in materials, moulds, or a photography setup.

If that estimate is inflated — because, say, a top seller in the niche gets a lot of repeat buyers who do not leave reviews, or because the model is calibrated on higher-volume categories — you can end up misjudging demand.

Conversely, if estimates are conservative, you might walk away from a genuinely good niche because the numbers looked underwhelming.

Neither outcome is the tool's fault exactly. The tools are doing their best with the data available. But it is worth knowing what you are working with.

What Is Actually on the Page (and Why It Matters)

When you run a search on Etsy, the results page contains a meaningful amount of real, unambiguous information:

  • Prices — exactly what each seller is charging
  • Review counts — how many reviews a listing has accumulated
  • Ad density — how many of the results are paid placements versus organic
  • Listing age signals — sometimes visible through review dates
  • Seller badges — Star Seller status, bestseller tags

None of this requires estimation. It is right there. And when you aggregate it across a full page of results, you get a genuinely useful picture of a niche: how competitive the pricing is, whether the top results are dominated by a handful of well-reviewed shops, how much of the page has been bought rather than earned.

That last point — ad density — is particularly underrated. A search result where most of the top spots are ads tells you something different about organic opportunity than one where the top results are all high-review organic listings. You cannot get that signal from a keyword volume figure alone.

Where eRank's Free Tier Fits (and Where It Does Not)

eRank's free plan limits the number of searches and lookups you can do per day, which is fair enough for a freemium product. For sellers who are already established and doing regular keyword research, the paid tiers are probably worth evaluating on their own merits.

But for someone in the earlier stage — just trying to sense-check whether a product idea has room in the market before making anything — the free tier can feel either too limited or too focused on search volume data that requires some experience to interpret well.

Search volume tells you how often a phrase is typed into Etsy. It does not tell you whether the existing results are beatable, whether the price point is sustainable, or whether the niche is flooded with ads. For that, you need to look at the actual search results.

How to Use Both Approaches Together

A practical workflow for validating a new product idea might look like this:

  • Use eRank or a similar tool to find keyword phrases with reasonable search volume in your category
  • Take those keywords to Etsy and run the actual searches
  • Use a page-level tool to read the real data: what are the price ranges, how many reviews do the top results have, how much of the page is paid placement
  • Make your judgement based on a combination of demand signals (search volume) and competition signals (real page data)

Neither data type alone gives you the full picture. Search volume without page-level competition data can lead you toward keywords that look good but are sewn up by established sellers. Page-level data without any sense of search volume might have you optimising for phrases nobody types.

The key is knowing which numbers are estimates and which are facts, so you weight them accordingly.
