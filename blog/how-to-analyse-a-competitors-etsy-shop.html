How to Analyse a Competitor's Etsy Shop: A Practical Guide

Whether you're testing a new product idea or trying to understand why another seller is doing better than you, looking at a competitor's Etsy shop is one of the most useful things you can do. The problem is knowing what to actually look at — and what to ignore.

This guide walks through a practical, honest approach to competitor research on Etsy. No invented sales figures, no guesswork. Just the data that's genuinely visible on the page and what it actually tells you.

What You Can Actually See on an Etsy Shop Page

Before diving into method, it's worth being clear about what Etsy makes public. When you visit a competitor's shop or search results page, you can see:

  • Listing prices and any sale discounts
  • Review counts on individual listings
  • The total number of shop reviews
  • How many sales a shop has made (shown on their profile)
  • Which listings carry a 'Bestseller' badge
  • How many listings are ads (marked 'Ad' in search results)
  • Listing photos, titles, and tags (some visible via the listing page)

That's a meaningful amount of information. The issue is that many sellers either overlook it entirely or get distracted by third-party tools that layer estimated figures on top — revenue projections, monthly sales guesses — that Etsy never actually publishes. Those estimates can be directionally useful, but they're models, not measurements. It's worth knowing the difference.

Start With the Search Results Page, Not the Shop

A common mistake is going straight to a competitor's shop profile. That gives you an overview, but it doesn't show you how their listings perform in the context of search — which is where most Etsy buyers find products.

Start by searching the keyword your customer would actually type. Look at which listings appear. Ask yourself:

  • How many of the top results are from the same seller? Dominating multiple positions is a sign of strong SEO or high relevance signals.
  • What proportion of results are ads? A search result page full of ads suggests this niche has strong commercial competition — sellers are paying to be visible because organic ranking is hard.
  • Where do your competitor's listings appear without ads? Organic position tells you more about genuine algorithmic strength than paid placement does.

Ad density is a particularly underrated signal. If you count the ads on a search page and find that the majority of visible listings are paid, that tells you the organic competition is fierce. If most results are organic, there may be more room to compete without a big ad spend.

How to Read a Competitor's Listing in Detail

Once you've identified listings worth examining, open them individually. Here's what to look at:

Review count and recency

A listing with many reviews has been selling for a while. But the number alone doesn't tell you much — a listing with 400 reviews accumulated over five years is a very different situation from one with 400 reviews in six months. Look at the dates of the most recent reviews to get a rough sense of current momentum. If reviews have dried up over the past year, that's worth noting.

Price positioning

Note the price, and note whether it's discounted. Some sellers run permanent 'sale' prices as a psychological tactic. Look at the original price shown alongside the sale price — that gap tells you something about their margin strategy and how they want to be perceived.

Compare prices across several competing listings for the same type of product. You'll usually find a clear price range with outliers at the top and bottom. Where you sit in that range is a deliberate choice, not an accident.

Listing title and photos

Titles reveal what keywords a seller is targeting. You don't need any tool to read a title. Look at how they've structured it — product type first, then attributes, then use case or audience. Compare that to your own titles.

Photos are often the real differentiator. Etsy is a visual platform. If a competitor's photos are significantly better than yours, that alone can explain a ranking or conversion gap.

Bestseller badge

The Bestseller badge appears when a listing has sold well relative to others in the shop. It's a shop-relative signal, not an absolute one — a shop with modest overall sales can still show the badge on its top performer. Treat it as a sign that this listing converts, not necessarily that it dominates the whole market.

Analysing a Competitor's Full Shop

Once you've looked at individual listings, visit their shop profile. Look at:

Total sales and total reviews

Sales count is public. It tells you roughly how established the shop is. A shop with tens of thousands of sales has compounding advantages — strong review history, Etsy algorithm trust, established repeat buyers. A newer shop with a few hundred sales is a much more instructive comparison point if you're just starting out.

Breadth of product range

How many listings do they have? Are they focused tightly on one niche or spread across many product types? A tightly focused shop often signals a seller who has found what works and doubled down on it. A very broad shop may be experimenting or running a print-on-demand operation.

Shop policies and presentation

Read their About section, policies, and any shop announcements. This isn't just background reading. It tells you what they emphasise to buyers — turnaround times, personalisation options, quality claims. If every top competitor leads with fast dispatch, and you don't, that's worth knowing.

What Third-Party Tools Add (and Where to Be Careful)

Tools like EverBee, eRank, and Alura can surface additional data points — keyword search volumes, estimated monthly sales, revenue projections. These are useful for getting a broad directional sense of a niche. But they're estimates built from models, not direct Etsy data. Etsy does not publish individual listing sales figures or revenue numbers.

That distinction matters when you're making real business decisions. If a tool tells you a competitor is making a certain amount per month, that's a calculated guess — it could be close, or it could be significantly off. Relying heavily on those figures can lead you to enter a niche that looks lucrative on paper but is harder in practice, or to avoid one that actually has room.

The most reliable competitor data is what's actually on the page. Review counts, prices, ad density, listing quality — these are observable facts, not estimates.

Turning the Analysis Into a Decision

Competitor research is only useful if it changes what you do. After analysing a competitor's shop, you should be able to answer:

  • Are they winning on price, on quality, on SEO, or on reviews — or some combination?
  • Is there a gap in price range, quality tier, or product variation that they're not covering?
  • Are their top listings genuinely strong, or are they benefiting from low competition in an underserved niche?
  • What would a buyer see if they compared your listing to theirs side by side?

The last question is often the most clarifying. Put your listing next to a top competitor's and look at it honestly — price, photos, title, review count. What would make a buyer choose yours?

A Simple Repeatable Process

Here's a straightforward routine you can run for any niche or competitor:

  • Search your target keyword and record the top ten organic results (ignoring ads for now)
  • Note which sellers appear more than once
  • Count the ads on the page as a proportion of total visible listings
  • Open the top three or four listings and record price, review count, and recent review dates
  • Visit each seller's shop and note total sales and number of listings
  • Compare listing photos and titles against your own
  • Identify one specific thing each competitor does better than you and one thing you could do better than them

Done consistently — even just once a month for your main niche — this builds a much clearer picture than any single snapshot.

FAQ: Analysing a Competitor's Etsy Shop

Can you see how many sales a competitor's Etsy listing has made?

Not directly for individual listings. Etsy shows a shop's total sales count on the profile page, but it does not publicly display per-listing sales figures. Review counts on individual listings give you a rough proxy — more reviews generally means more sales — but the ratio varies. Third-party tools estimate per-listing sales, but these are models rather than real data.

Is it against Etsy's rules to look at a competitor's shop?

No. Browsing any public Etsy shop or search page is entirely fine. All the information discussed in this guide is publicly visible to any shopper. You're not accessing anything private or restricted by looking at prices, titles, reviews, or photos on a competitor's listings.

How do I know if a niche is too competitive on Etsy?

There is no single threshold, but useful signals include: a high proportion of ads on the search results page (suggesting sellers need to pay to be seen), review counts in the thousands on top listings (indicating long-established competition), and little price variation (meaning there's limited room to differentiate on value). Conversely, niches where top listings have modest review counts and mix of ad and organic results often have more room for a new entrant.

Do I need to pay for a tool to research Etsy competitors?

No. Most of the most useful competitive data is visible for free on Etsy itself — prices, reviews, ad placements, titles, and photos. Paid tools add estimated figures and keyword data that can be useful for broader research, but they're not essential for understanding what a specific competitor is doing well. Starting with what's genuinely on the page is both free and more reliable.
