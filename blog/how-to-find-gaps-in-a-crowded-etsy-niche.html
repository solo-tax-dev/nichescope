How to Find Gaps in a Crowded Etsy Niche

If you search for almost anything on Etsy right now, you will find hundreds of listings. Personalised gifts, digital planners, candles, pet portraits — the shelves are full. That does not mean there is no room for you. It means you need to look more carefully at what is actually there before you commit time and money to a new product.

This guide walks through a practical method for spotting gaps in a crowded niche using data you can see with your own eyes — not estimated figures that no one can verify.

Why 'Crowded' Does Not Always Mean 'No Opportunity'

A niche with a lot of listings is not automatically a niche to avoid. High listing counts can actually signal healthy demand. The more useful question is whether the existing sellers are serving buyers well, or whether there are underserved corners of the market.

Gaps can exist even in the busiest categories. They tend to show up as:

  • Price clusters with nothing in between — lots of very cheap listings and a few luxury ones, but nothing mid-range
  • Listing pages dominated by ads, which pushes organic results lower and creates room for well-optimised listings
  • Popular search terms where most results have very few reviews, suggesting the niche is growing faster than sellers have caught up
  • Variations nobody is making — a particular colour, size, material or occasion that buyers search for but struggle to find

None of these gaps require secret data. They are visible if you know where to look.

Start With the Search Results Page, Not a Tool

Before reaching for any research tool, spend time actually browsing Etsy as a buyer would. Type in the phrase a customer might use. Scroll through the first two or three pages of results. Notice the shape of what you see.

Ask yourself:

  • How many of the results are ads versus organic listings?
  • Do most listings look similar, or is there variety in style, price and format?
  • Are the top listings covered in reviews, or do even the first-page results have relatively few?
  • What price range dominates, and where are the gaps?

Writing down what you observe — even in a simple spreadsheet — gives you a factual baseline. This takes longer than clicking a button in a research tool, but it grounds your thinking in what is genuinely there.

How to Read Price Distribution for Gaps

Price is one of the clearest signals of a gap. Open a search, sort by relevance (the default), and note the prices of the first twenty or thirty listings. Group them roughly into low, mid and high.

Common patterns you might find:

  • Race to the bottom: Almost everything is priced very cheaply, often because sellers are competing on price rather than differentiation. If you can offer more perceived value — better photos, clearer personalisation, faster dispatch — there may be room at a slightly higher price point.
  • Luxury gap: Lots of budget options but nothing premium. Some buyers actively want to spend more on a gift and struggle to find something that feels worth it.
  • Missing mid-range: Budget and premium both exist, but there is a gap in the middle where most buyers actually sit. That gap is often an opportunity.

You do not need sales estimates for this. The prices are right there on the page.

Review Counts Tell You About Momentum, Not Just Popularity

Reviews on Etsy are public. You can see how many reviews each listing has, which is a proxy for how established a seller is in that niche — though it is not a direct measure of recent sales.

What to look for:

  • First-page results with low review counts: If the top-ranked listings for a keyword have only a handful of reviews each, that niche may be easier to enter than it looks. New listings can compete without needing thousands of sales behind them.
  • Consistent high review counts: If every first-page result has hundreds or thousands of reviews, you are looking at an established niche. That does not rule it out, but your differentiation needs to be sharper.
  • Mixed review counts: A mix — some listings with many reviews, some with very few — suggests the niche is active enough for newcomers to get traction.

The important caveat: review count is not the same as sales volume. A listing could have few reviews because buyers rarely leave them, or because it is relatively new. Use review counts as one signal among several, not as a definitive answer.

Ad Density as a Gap Signal

When a search results page is heavily loaded with ads, it tells you two things. First, sellers in that niche are willing to pay to appear at the top, which suggests they are making sales. Second, organic results are being pushed down the page, which means organic competition for visibility is different from what the listing count alone implies.

A niche with high ad density but lower organic competition can sometimes be easier to rank in organically than it first appears — because many of the top spots are paid, and the organic results sitting below them face less direct competition from established sellers.

Narrowing Down: Long-Tail Searches and Occasion Gaps

Broad keywords are crowded by definition. The gaps are almost always in narrower searches.

Instead of searching for 'personalised mug', try 'personalised mug for new dad' or 'personalised mug funny retirement'. These longer, more specific searches often have far fewer results — and if demand is there, that is a genuine gap.

Occasion gaps are particularly common. Sellers tend to cluster around the biggest occasions (Christmas, birthdays, Mother's Day) and overlook smaller ones that still attract consistent searches: promotions, housewarming gifts for renters, milestone anniversaries for specific years. Browse buyer-facing gift guides and wishlist content to spot occasions that have buyer intent but thin supply on Etsy.

Checking Format and Variation Gaps

Even within a well-served niche, specific formats or variations may be missing. Look at what the top listings offer and ask what they do not offer:

  • Are all the prints landscape format when some buyers clearly want portrait?
  • Does every candle come in one size when buyers might want a travel tin?
  • Are all the personalised items offering the same two or three name styles?
  • Is every digital download a PDF when some buyers might prefer an editable format?

These are gaps you can only spot by looking carefully at what is actually available, not by looking at aggregate numbers.

A Word on Estimated Sales Data

Several popular Etsy research tools — eRank, EverBee, Alura and others — show estimated monthly sales or revenue figures for listings and shops. These estimates are generated by the tools themselves. Etsy does not publish individual listing sales data. The estimates are the tools' best guesses based on various signals, and different tools often produce different figures for the same listing.

That does not mean those tools are useless — keyword data and trend tracking can be genuinely helpful. But if you are making a product decision based on an estimated sales number, it is worth knowing that the number is an estimate, not a verified figure.

Putting It Together: A Simple Gap-Finding Process

Here is a repeatable process you can use before committing to a new product:

  • Search your target keyword on Etsy and note the listing count
  • Record the price range of the first twenty to thirty results — look for clusters and gaps
  • Check review counts across first-page listings — are they mostly high, low or mixed?
  • Count the ad density — how many of the first page results are promoted?
  • Try three or four long-tail variations of your keyword and repeat the above
  • Note any format or occasion variations that appear underserved

This process takes thirty to sixty minutes per niche. It is slower than clicking a dashboard, but the conclusions are based on what is actually there.

FAQ

How do I know if an Etsy niche is too crowded to enter?

Listing count alone is not a reliable measure. A better signal is whether first-page results have consistently high review counts (suggesting entrenched sellers) and whether there are clear price or format gaps you could fill. A niche with many listings but thin reviews and scattered pricing is often more accessible than one with fewer listings all dominated by high-review shops.

Can I trust estimated sales figures from Etsy research tools?

They can be useful as a rough directional signal, but Etsy does not publish individual listing or shop sales data. The figures shown by tools like EverBee or eRank are estimates generated by those tools, not verified numbers. Different tools often show different figures for the same listing. Use them as one input, not as a firm basis for a product decision.

What does ad density on an Etsy search page tell me?

It tells you how many sellers in that niche are paying for promoted placement. High ad density can mean the niche is commercially viable (sellers are spending money, which implies returns) but can also mean organic results are pushed down. In some cases, high ad density with lower organic competition means it is easier to rank organically than the total listing count suggests.

Do I need to pay for an Etsy research tool to find niche gaps?

No. Most of the signals that matter — price distribution, review counts, ad density — are visible on the Etsy search results page without any tool. A free tool like NicheScope can make reading that data faster, but the underlying method works without it. The main thing paid tools add is keyword volume estimates, which are useful but not essential for gap-finding.
