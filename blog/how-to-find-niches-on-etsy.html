How to Find Niches on Etsy Using Real, Visible Data

Most advice about finding Etsy niches tells you to chase sales numbers. The problem is that Etsy does not publish sales figures. Any tool that shows you a listing's monthly sales or revenue is working from an estimate — a model built on assumptions. Those estimates can be useful, but they are not facts, and basing a product decision on them carries real risk.

There is another way. Etsy's search results pages are full of publicly visible signals that tell you a great deal about competition and demand without requiring a single guessed number. This article walks you through how to read those signals methodically, so you can make sourcing and pricing decisions on evidence rather than estimates.

Why Publicly Visible Data Is Enough

Before diving into method, it is worth being clear about what you actually need to know when evaluating a niche:

  • Is there genuine buyer demand, or is it a ghost market with no real activity?
  • How strong is the existing competition?
  • Are established sellers dominating paid placements, pushing organic listings down?
  • What price range do buyers actually accept?

None of these questions require a sales estimate. They can all be answered — imperfectly but honestly — from data that is sitting in plain sight on every Etsy search results page.

Signal 1: Review Counts as a Proxy for Sales Activity

Etsy buyers are not required to leave a review, and a meaningful percentage never do. But reviews are public, cumulative, and real. A listing with several hundred reviews has demonstrably sold a lot of units. A listing with two reviews might have sold two items or two hundred — you cannot tell. The signal becomes useful in aggregate.

What to look for in a search results page

When you run a search on Etsy, scan the review counts across the first two or three pages of results. Ask yourself:

  • Are review counts clustered at the top? If the first few listings have thousands of reviews and everything else has under ten, the niche has one or two dominant players and very little oxygen for newcomers.
  • Are review counts spread more evenly? When many listings on a page each have a few dozen to a few hundred reviews, buyers are distributing purchases across multiple sellers. That is usually a healthier environment to enter.
  • How many listings have zero reviews? A search page full of listings with no reviews at all could mean the niche is brand new, or it could mean buyers are searching but not buying. Either way, it is a yellow flag worth investigating further.

The pattern across a full page matters more than any single listing. One viral listing with ten thousand reviews tells you less than noticing that the average listing on the page has around forty reviews.

Signal 2: Ad Density — Who Is Paying to Compete Here?

Etsy's search results mix organic listings with paid adverts. Etsy labels these, though not always prominently. Counting how many of the first sixteen or so results are ads gives you a quick read on how commercially competitive a niche is.

High ad density: what it means

When most of the top spots are paid placements, it tells you that sellers in this niche are willing to spend money to maintain visibility. That is not necessarily a reason to avoid the niche, but it does mean two things: organic rankings are harder to reach, and your competitors are likely operating on margins that support ad spend. If you cannot absorb that cost, you start at a structural disadvantage.

Low ad density: what it means

A search page where very few or no results are ads suggests sellers in this niche are not bidding heavily for traffic. This could mean the niche is less competitive, or that profit margins are too thin to make ads worthwhile, or simply that sellers here have not adopted paid promotion. Combined with healthy review counts spread across multiple sellers, low ad density is one of the clearest signals that a niche is genuinely accessible.

Signal 3: Real Prices, Not Revenue Guesses

Pricing is one of the most practically useful things you can extract from a search results page, and it is entirely accurate because the prices are right there, publicly displayed.

Building a real price map

Rather than noting the cheapest and most expensive listings and assuming the truth is somewhere in the middle, it is more useful to look at where most listings cluster. If the majority of listings on a page are priced between £12 and £22, and two outliers are at £6 and £55, the market is telling you the acceptable range is roughly £12–£22. The outliers exist, but they are not defining buyer expectations.

Cross this price map with the review distribution. If the listings with the most reviews are in the mid-price range rather than at the bottom, that is a strong indicator that buyers in this niche are not simply choosing the cheapest option. That is exactly the kind of niche where a new seller can compete on quality and presentation rather than racing to the lowest price.

Spotting a race to the bottom

Conversely, if the listings with the highest review counts are consistently the cheapest listings on the page, you are looking at a price-sensitive niche where volume is the main lever. Unless you have a genuine cost advantage, that is a difficult place to build a sustainable shop.

Putting the Three Signals Together

No single signal is conclusive on its own. The useful insight comes from reading all three together for a given search query. Here is a practical framework:

  • Accessible niche indicator: Review counts spread across many listings (not monopolised by one or two), low ad density, and price clustering in a range that leaves room for margin.
  • Saturated niche indicator: A handful of listings with enormous review counts, high ad density, and price pressure at the low end.
  • Uncertain / emerging niche indicator: Many listings with zero or very few reviews, low ad density, and inconsistent pricing. Worth monitoring over weeks rather than acting on immediately.

This framework does not tell you exactly how many sales you will make. It was never going to. What it does is give you an honest picture of the competitive landscape before you invest time or money into a product.

A Note on Repeating Searches Over Time

A single snapshot of a search page is useful, but two snapshots taken a month apart are more useful. If review counts are growing across many listings in a niche, that is evidence of sustained buyer demand. If the same listings look identical a month later, the niche may be stagnant. This is free to do with any tool or with no tool at all — it just requires the habit of checking back.

Many sellers do their initial niche research and then never revisit it. Building a short list of searches to recheck periodically is one of the most underused practices in Etsy product research.

FAQ

Do I need sales data to find a good Etsy niche?

Not necessarily. Review counts, ad density, and real prices can tell you a great deal about competition and buyer behaviour without requiring any sales estimates. Sales estimates add context, but they are modelled figures, not facts published by Etsy.

How many results should I look at when researching a niche?

The first two to three pages of results (roughly thirty to fifty listings) gives a reasonable picture of the competitive landscape. Beyond that, you are looking at listings Etsy considers less relevant to the search, which tells you less about who you would actually be competing with.

What does high ad density on Etsy search actually mean for a new seller?

It means established sellers are paying to hold their visibility. As a new seller without an ad budget, your organic listings would appear below those paid results. It does not make a niche impossible to enter, but it does raise the bar for getting seen without spending on ads yourself.
