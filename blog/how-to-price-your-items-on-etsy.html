How to Price Your Items on Etsy: A Practical Guide

Pricing on Etsy is one of those decisions that feels straightforward until you actually sit down to do it. Too low and you undercut yourself; too high and you wonder why nothing sells. Most advice online gives you a formula and calls it done. This guide goes further — it covers the formula, but also how to use real market data to sense-check your numbers before you commit.

Start With Your Costs — All of Them

Before you look at what anyone else is charging, you need to know your own floor price: the minimum you can charge and still make money. A lot of sellers skip this step or do it loosely, then wonder why a busy shop still feels unprofitable.

Add up everything that goes into one unit:

  • Materials and supplies — the actual cost per item, not per bulk order (divide your bulk cost by the number of units you get from it)
  • Labour — your time, valued at a real hourly rate you would actually accept
  • Packaging — boxes, tissue paper, stickers, inserts
  • Etsy fees — listing fee (currently 20p/20¢ per listing), transaction fee (6.5% of the sale price including postage), and payment processing (varies by country)
  • Postage costs — if you offer free postage, this comes out of your margin
  • Overheads — a proportional share of any software, studio rent, or equipment costs

Once you have a total cost per unit, add the profit margin you actually need. A common starting point is to aim for at least 30% margin after all costs, but that depends on your product category and how much competition you face.

Why Undercutting the Cheapest Listing Is Usually a Mistake

It is tempting to find the lowest price in your category and go just below it. That strategy tends to attract buyers who will leave the moment someone goes even lower — and it sets a price anchor that is very hard to move up later.

A better approach is to understand where your product sits in the market and price to match that positioning. A handmade ceramic mug with beautiful product photography and glowing reviews can charge considerably more than a plain one with a blurry photo. The price signals the quality, and buyers read it that way.

How to Research What Competitors Are Actually Charging

This is where most pricing guides get vague. They say 'look at competitors' without explaining how to do that in a way that gives you useful, reliable data.

What you can see directly on Etsy

When you search a keyword on Etsy, the results show you listing prices, review counts, and which listings are ads. That is genuinely useful information. A listing with hundreds of reviews at a certain price point is a strong signal that buyers accept that price. A search page full of paid ads tells you competition is high enough that sellers are spending money to stay visible.

What research tools add — and what to watch out for

Tools like EverBee, eRank, and Alura overlay additional data on Etsy search results. Some of that is helpful. However, most of these tools also show estimated monthly sales and revenue figures. It is worth knowing that Etsy does not publish sales data — those figures are calculated estimates, not confirmed numbers. They can be directionally useful, but treating them as precise facts can lead you to price against a competitor's apparent success that may not reflect reality.

What to actually look for when researching competitors

  • Price range across the first page — what is the floor, the ceiling, and where does the cluster sit?
  • Review counts at different price points — are the higher-priced listings still getting buyers?
  • Ad density — how many of the top results are paid? High ad density can mean organic visibility is harder, which affects how aggressively you need to price to get early traction.
  • Listing quality — photography, descriptions, titles. If competing listings are weak, there may be room to charge more and win on presentation.

Psychological Pricing and What Works on Etsy

Etsy buyers are not purely rational. A few pricing patterns are worth being aware of.

Charm pricing

Prices ending in .99 or .95 are associated with value and deals. Prices ending in round numbers (£30, $45) often feel more premium. Neither is universally better — match the approach to how you want your shop to be perceived.

Anchoring with bundles

Offering a single item and a bundle of three creates a reference point. The single item no longer looks expensive against the bundle, and buyers who want value will upgrade themselves. This also increases your average order value without requiring you to change your per-unit price.

Free postage vs. showing postage separately

Etsy has encouraged sellers to offer free postage because buyers respond better to it in search. If you go this route, build the postage cost into your item price rather than absorbing it — otherwise you are reducing your margin on every sale.

When to Raise Your Prices

Many Etsy sellers underprice for years out of fear that raising prices will kill sales. In practice, a modest price increase often has little effect on conversion — and sometimes improves it, because a higher price can signal higher quality.

Good times to revisit your prices:

  • When your material or postage costs go up
  • When you have accumulated enough reviews to justify a premium
  • When you are consistently sold out or on a long backlog
  • When you launch improved photography or packaging
  • Annually, as a general review — costs rarely go down over time

If you are nervous about raising prices, test it on one or two listings first. Watch the conversion rate over a few weeks. You may find that nothing changes, or that you sell slightly fewer items but at a better margin overall.

Using Real Market Data Before You Launch a New Product

If you are still in the product validation stage — deciding whether to make something at all — pricing research is part of that process. The question is not just 'can I sell this?' but 'can I sell this at a price that makes sense for my costs?'

Look at the search results for your target keyword. If the price range on the first page is well below what you would need to charge to make a margin, that is important information before you invest in materials and time. Equally, if high-review listings are charging considerably more than you expected, there may be more room in the market than you assumed.

A Simple Pricing Checklist

  • Calculate your true cost per unit including labour and fees
  • Add a margin that makes the business sustainable
  • Check what comparable listings on the first page are charging
  • Note where the reviewed, established listings sit — that is often your target range
  • Decide where your product sits in terms of quality and presentation, and price accordingly
  • Factor postage into the item price if you plan to offer free postage
  • Set a reminder to review prices at least once a year

FAQ: How to Price Your Items on Etsy

What percentage should I add for profit on Etsy?

There is no single right answer, but many sellers aim for a margin of at least 30% after all costs — materials, labour, packaging, and Etsy fees. In practice, the margin you need depends on your volume. If you sell a high volume of lower-priced items, even a smaller margin per unit can add up. If you make bespoke or time-intensive products, you may need a higher margin to make the time worthwhile. The key is to know your actual cost per unit before setting any figure.

Should I offer free postage on Etsy?

Etsy has generally encouraged free postage because buyers respond well to it in search results. Whether it makes sense for you depends on your product weight and where your buyers are located. If you do offer free postage, build the cost into your item price rather than absorbing it — otherwise it quietly reduces your margin on every order.

How do I know if my Etsy prices are competitive?

Search your main keyword on Etsy and look at what the first page of results is charging — particularly the listings with strong review counts, since those have proven buyer acceptance at their price point. Pay attention to where the majority of listings cluster, and where the outliers (very cheap and very expensive) sit. Also note how many results are ads, which tells you something about how competitive organic visibility is in that niche.

Can I charge more than everyone else on Etsy?

Yes, but you need a clear reason for buyers to pay the premium. Better photography, stronger reviews, a more polished brand, faster dispatch, or genuinely higher quality materials can all justify a higher price. Charging more without a visible differentiator tends not to work — but charging more because your listing is demonstrably better often does. Look at what the top-reviewed listings in your category charge before assuming the market will not support a higher price.
