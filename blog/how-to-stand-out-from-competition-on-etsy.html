How to Stand Out From Competition on Etsy: What the Search Results Actually Tell You

Most advice about standing out on Etsy starts with a bold claim: find a niche with high sales and low competition. Simple enough — except Etsy does not publish sales data. The numbers you see in most research tools are estimates built from indirect signals, not figures Etsy has released. That gap between what sellers think they know and what is actually knowable causes a lot of wasted effort.

This article is about what you can know for certain when you look at an Etsy search results page — and how to use that information to make smarter decisions about positioning, pricing, and whether a niche is worth entering at all.

Why 'Beat the Competition' Is the Wrong Starting Question

When sellers ask how to stand out from competition on Etsy, they usually mean one of two things: either they want to know if a niche is crowded, or they want to understand why certain listings rank above theirs. Both are reasonable questions, but they get muddled when the data you are working from is uncertain.

If a tool tells you a competitor is making a certain amount per month, and you price or position your product in response to that figure, you are making a real business decision based on an estimate. That estimate might be directionally useful. It might also be significantly off. The problem is you often cannot tell which.

A more reliable approach is to look only at what the search results page actually shows you — and there is more useful information there than most sellers realise.

What an Etsy Search Page Actually Tells You

Open any Etsy search and you have immediate access to several concrete, verifiable data points. No tool required at first — just careful observation.

Prices across the results

Price is right there on every listing tile. Scan the first two pages of results for your target keyword and you will quickly see where the market clusters. Is everything priced between £8 and £15, with a few outliers at £30? That clustering tells you what buyers in this niche have been trained to expect. If you want to price above the cluster, you need a visible reason — better photography, a clearer value proposition, a meaningfully different product.

Review counts

Review counts are public. A listing with 4,000 reviews has been selling for a long time, almost certainly. A listing with 12 reviews might be newer, or it might simply be a weaker listing. Neither tells you exactly how many sales occurred, but together they tell you something about the competitive landscape — which sellers have had time to build social proof, and which have not.

If the top ten results in your niche all have thousands of reviews, that is meaningful. It does not mean you cannot compete, but it does mean buyers have a lot of established options to choose from. You will need a clearer reason to stand out — not just a slightly lower price.

Ad density

Etsy marks paid listings with a small 'Ad' label. If you scroll through the first page and a large proportion of the tiles are ads, that tells you sellers in this niche are investing in paid placement to stay visible. It could mean organic ranking is harder to achieve. It could also mean the niche is profitable enough that sellers are willing to spend. Either way, it is a signal worth noting.

Conversely, if you see almost no ads, that might mean the niche is not commercially attractive — or it might mean sellers have not yet discovered that paid ads work there. Context matters.

Listing quality signals

Look at the photography, the titles, the thumbnail choices. If the majority of listings look similar — same angle, same plain background, same style of title — there is a visual gap you might be able to fill. Standing out from competition on Etsy is sometimes as simple as being the listing that looks different enough to make someone pause.

What the Search Results Cannot Tell You (And Why That Matters)

Review counts give you a rough proxy for historical sales activity, but they do not tell you conversion rates, average order values, return rates, or how much any given shop is currently earning. Several popular Etsy research tools — EverBee, eRank, Alura and others — offer estimated sales and revenue figures. These estimates are based on their own modelling; Etsy has not released the underlying data. They can be a useful starting point for forming a hypothesis, but they should not be treated as confirmed figures.

This distinction matters most when you are deciding whether to invest time and money creating a new product line. If you are going to spend weeks making samples, writing listings, and setting up photography, you want to base that decision on something more solid than a revenue estimate that could be substantially inaccurate in either direction.

The safer approach is to treat search result data as your primary evidence, and treat any estimated figures as a secondary, uncertain input.

A Practical Framework for Reading Competition

Here is a straightforward process you can use before launching or repositioning a product.

Step 1: Search for your exact target keyword

Use the keyword a buyer would actually type. Look at the first two pages of results. You want a realistic picture of what a buyer sees when they search for what you are selling.

Step 2: Note the price range

Write down the lowest, highest, and most common price point. Where do you plan to sit, and why? If you cannot articulate a reason for your price relative to the spread, that is worth thinking through before you publish.

Step 3: Count review clusters

How many listings have over 1,000 reviews? How many have under 50? A results page dominated by high-review listings signals an established, competitive niche. A mix of high and low suggests there is still space for newer sellers to gain traction.

Step 4: Check ad density

Count how many of the first 20 results are ads. A high proportion suggests sellers are relying on paid visibility. A low proportion suggests organic ranking is more achievable — or that the niche has less commercial interest.

Step 5: Identify the visual gap

Look at the thumbnails as a grid. What does the page look like overall? Where is the visual sameness, and where might a different approach get attention?

Using This Data to Actually Differentiate

Once you have a clear read on what the search results show, differentiation becomes a more concrete problem.

If prices are tightly clustered and review counts are high across the board, competing on price alone is unlikely to work. Buyers already have trusted, well-reviewed options at established prices. You need to offer something visibly different — a material, a customisation option, a presentation style, a more specific niche angle.

If ad density is high and organic results have moderate review counts, there may be an opportunity to invest in SEO and photography to earn organic placement that more established sellers are not bothering to maintain.

If the top results all look visually similar, even modest improvements to photography or listing structure can make your tile stand out in the grid — which affects click-through before anyone has even read your title.

None of this requires knowing how much a competitor earns. It requires looking carefully at what is in front of you.

Before You Make the Product: Validate First

This analysis is arguably more valuable before you create a product than after. Many sellers build inventory, photograph it, write the listings — and then research competition. By that point, they are looking for confirmation rather than information.

If you spend twenty minutes on the search results page before committing to a product, you will know whether the niche is saturated with highly-reviewed shops, whether your planned price is in line with market expectations, and whether there is a visual or positioning gap worth pursuing. That is a much better use of your research time.

FAQ

How do I know if an Etsy niche is too competitive to enter?

There is no single threshold, but useful signals include: most top results have very high review counts (suggesting established sellers with strong social proof), prices are tightly clustered with little room for differentiation, and ad density is high (suggesting organic ranking is expensive to achieve). A niche where a few listings dominate is harder to enter than one where results are more spread across different shops and review counts.

Do I need to know a competitor's sales figures to compete with them?

Not really. Sales estimates can suggest whether a niche has commercial activity, but they do not tell you why a listing is selling — which is the actually useful question. Visible factors like price, photography quality, review count, and search relevance are more directly actionable than a revenue estimate.

What does high ad density on Etsy search results mean?

It means sellers in that niche are paying for placement rather than (or in addition to) relying on organic ranking. This can indicate a competitive niche where organic visibility is hard to achieve, or simply that sellers in that category have adopted ads as a standard practice. It is worth noting as one signal among several, not treating it as definitive on its own.
