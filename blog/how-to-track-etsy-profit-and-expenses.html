How to Track Etsy Profit and Expenses: A Plain Guide

Most Etsy sellers start tracking money only after something goes wrong — a tax bill they weren't ready for, a product that looked profitable but wasn't, or a month where revenue looked fine but the bank balance didn't agree. This guide walks through how to track Etsy profit and expenses properly, from the basics of what to record to the tools that actually help.

Why Tracking Matters More on Etsy Than You Might Expect

Etsy's fee structure is more complex than a single percentage. You're dealing with listing fees, transaction fees, payment processing fees, offsite ad fees when they apply, and any Etsy Plus subscription if you have one. On top of that, you have your own costs: materials, packaging, postage, software subscriptions, photography, and your time if you choose to value it.

A sale that brings in £30 can look very different once all those layers come off. Sellers who don't track the detail often discover too late that a product they've been making for months is barely breaking even — or actually losing money after postage is included.

What to Record for Every Sale

You don't need complicated software to start. A basic spreadsheet works for most small shops. The key is being consistent about what you capture.

Income to record

  • Item price paid by the buyer — what they actually paid, not your listed price
  • Postage charged to the buyer — this counts as income even if it just covers your costs
  • Any gift wrapping or personalisation add-ons

Fees and deductions to record

  • Listing fee — currently charged per item listed and per sale of a multi-quantity listing
  • Transaction fee — Etsy takes a percentage of the total sale including postage
  • Payment processing fee — charged through Etsy Payments; varies slightly by country
  • Offsite Ads fee — only applies when a sale comes through an offsite ad; the rate depends on your shop's total sales history
  • Etsy Ads spend — if you run on-platform ads, track daily or weekly spend separately

Cost of goods to record

  • Materials used per item (break bulk purchases down to a per-unit cost)
  • Packaging materials per order
  • Postage paid to the carrier — not the amount you charged, the amount you actually spent
  • Any fees for tools or subscriptions used specifically for your shop

How to Get Your Etsy Fee Data

Etsy provides a monthly statement in your shop's Finance section. You can download your payment account CSV, which lists every transaction including fees broken out by type. This is the most accurate source for your actual fee costs — it reflects what Etsy actually charged you, not an estimate.

Download it at the end of each month and match it against your own records. Any discrepancy is worth investigating, because Etsy occasionally applies the offsite ads fee in ways sellers don't immediately notice.

Calculating Actual Profit Per Item

Once you have the numbers, the calculation is straightforward:

Profit = Sale price (inc. postage charged) − Etsy fees − Cost of goods − Actual postage paid − Proportion of any fixed overheads

That last item — fixed overheads — is where people often stop short. If you pay for a label printer, a Canva subscription, or a storage unit, those costs belong in your figures too, spread across the number of orders you fulfil.

Some sellers also factor in their own time at an hourly rate. This is optional, but it's worth doing at least once per product type to understand whether the shop is actually sustainable.

Tools That Can Help

Spreadsheets

Google Sheets or Excel work well for most small Etsy shops. Build a simple template with columns for date, item, sale price, postage charged, fees, materials cost, postage paid, and net profit. Colour-code the profit column so losses are obvious. Once the template is built, entering data takes a few minutes per week.

Accounting software

If your shop is growing, or if you sell on multiple platforms, dedicated software like Wave (free), QuickBooks, or Xero can connect to your bank account and make reconciliation easier. Some also have Etsy integrations that pull transaction data automatically, though you should always check the fee breakdown matches your actual Etsy statement.

Etsy's own finance tools

Etsy's built-in analytics show your revenue over time, but they don't subtract your costs or give you a profit figure. They're useful for spotting trends — which products sell most, which months are quieter — but they're not a substitute for tracking your actual costs yourself.

How Competition Research Connects to Pricing — and Profit

Tracking your costs tells you what you need to charge to be profitable. But whether you can actually charge that price depends on what the market will bear — and that's where understanding your competition becomes part of the same exercise.

If you're in a niche where most sellers are pricing at £12–£18 and your cost structure requires £22 to make a reasonable margin, you have a real problem that no amount of optimisation will fix. You either need to reduce costs, reposition the product, or find a less crowded niche where higher prices are normal.

Before setting prices (or before committing to a new product at all), it's worth checking what competitors are actually charging and how many reviews their listings have accumulated — both visible signals of how established the competition is. A free Etsy competition checker can pull that on-page data without requiring an account or any special permissions, so you can see real prices and review counts rather than estimated revenue figures that Etsy doesn't actually publish.

A Note on Estimated Sales Data from Third-Party Tools

Tools like EverBee, eRank, and Alura offer estimated monthly sales and revenue figures for Etsy listings. These estimates can be useful as a rough directional signal, but they're generated from modelling rather than from data Etsy actually provides — because Etsy doesn't publish sales volumes. Treat them as one input, not as verified fact, and don't base your cost and pricing decisions solely on estimated figures you can't verify.

The numbers you can verify are the ones on the page: listed prices, number of reviews, and whether a search result page is heavy with ads. Those visible signals, combined with your own careful cost tracking, give you a much more solid foundation.

Keeping Records for Tax

If you sell on Etsy and make a profit above the relevant threshold in your country, that income is taxable. In the UK, Etsy also reports seller data to HMRC under the digital platform reporting rules that came into effect in 2024, so the expectation that hobby sellers go unnoticed has largely gone.

Keep records of your income and expenses for at least five years in the UK (six years if you complete a self-assessment return late). Store your Etsy CSV statements somewhere safe alongside any receipts for materials and postage.

If you're unsure about your obligations, a session with an accountant who works with small creative businesses is worth the cost — it's also a deductible expense.

Getting Into a Routine

The sellers who find profit tracking least painful are the ones who make it a small regular habit rather than a quarterly panic. Set aside twenty minutes at the end of each week to update your records while the transactions are still fresh. At the end of each month, download your Etsy statement and reconcile. At the end of each quarter, review which products are genuinely profitable and which are costing you more than you're making.

It doesn't have to be elaborate. It just has to be consistent.

FAQ: Tracking Etsy Profit and Expenses

What expenses can Etsy sellers deduct?

Generally, any cost that is wholly and directly related to running your Etsy shop can be deducted from your taxable profit. This includes materials, packaging, postage, Etsy fees, advertising spend, relevant software subscriptions, a proportion of your broadband or phone bill if used for the business, and professional fees like accountancy. Keep receipts and records for everything. The rules vary slightly by country, so check with a local tax authority or accountant if you're unsure what applies to you.

Does Etsy provide a profit and loss statement?

No. Etsy provides a payment account statement that shows your sales income and the fees it has charged, but it does not subtract your cost of goods, postage paid, or other business expenses. You need to build your own profit and loss picture by combining your Etsy statement with your own cost records.

How do I work out my cost of goods per item?

For materials you buy in bulk, divide the total cost of the batch by the number of units it produces. For example, if you spend £40 on yarn that makes 20 items, your material cost per item is £2. Do the same for packaging. Add any direct labour cost if you're including your own time. Revisit these figures whenever your supplier prices change — material costs can creep up and quietly erode margins you thought were healthy.

Should I track Etsy income separately from other income?

Yes, keep Etsy income and expenses in their own records even if you also earn from other sources or platforms. It makes it much easier to assess which part of your business is profitable, to complete a tax return accurately, and to spot problems early. If you sell on multiple platforms, a separate tab or account for each one is a simple way to keep things clean.
