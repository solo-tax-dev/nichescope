Low Competition Etsy Niches Digital: How to Actually Find Them

If you sell digital products on Etsy — printables, templates, planners, SVGs, fonts, digital art — you have probably noticed that the obvious niches are crowded. Search for 'wedding invitation template' or 'budget planner printable' and you will find thousands of listings, many of them from shops with years of reviews behind them.

That does not mean there is nowhere left to go. It means you need a sharper way of looking. This article explains how to identify genuinely low competition digital niches on Etsy using data you can actually see, rather than estimates that may or may not reflect reality.

Why Digital Products and Etsy Are a Useful Combination

Digital products have a few characteristics that make niche research especially worthwhile. There is no inventory cost, so the downside of testing a new niche is low. Listings can be updated quickly if you want to adjust your positioning. And because buyers can filter by digital download, you can often evaluate a niche in isolation without physical products muddying the picture.

The flip side is that low barriers to entry mean niches fill up faster. A trending digital product format — say, a particular style of Notion template or a specific type of SVG cut file — can go from uncrowded to saturated within months. Timing and specificity matter more in digital than in most other Etsy categories.

What 'Low Competition' Actually Means on Etsy

It is worth being precise about this before going any further, because 'low competition' gets used loosely.

Result count alone is not the answer

A search that returns 800 results is not automatically easier to break into than one that returns 8,000. What matters more is the distribution of reviews and the pricing behaviour of the shops on the first page. If the top results all have hundreds or thousands of reviews, newer listings are unlikely to displace them quickly regardless of how the overall result count looks.

Review counts are a useful proxy

Reviews on Etsy are public. You can count them. A niche where the top listings have modest review counts — say, a handful to a few dozen — suggests the category has not yet been colonised by established shops. That does not guarantee sales, but it means the playing field is more level.

Ad density tells you something too

When you search a term on Etsy and the first two rows are all ads, that signals a commercially competitive niche. Sellers are willing to pay to appear there. In less competed niches, you will often see organic results much earlier in the page. This is visible data — you can see it yourself — and it is one of the cleaner signals available without needing any estimates.

How to Find Low Competition Digital Niches Systematically

There is no shortcut that reliably surfaces untapped niches without any effort. But there is a method that keeps you grounded in real signals rather than guesswork.

Start broad, then get specific

Pick a digital product format you can create — printables, Canva templates, SVG files, digital stickers, fonts, spreadsheets, whatever. Search that broad term on Etsy and observe the first page. Then start adding qualifiers: audience, occasion, style, software, use case. Each qualifier narrows the pool of competing listings and often reduces the average review count of what you find.

For example, 'planner printable' is broad and competitive. 'ADHD daily planner printable' is narrower. 'ADHD daily planner printable for adults with time blocking' is narrower still. At some point along that path, you will often find searches where the top results have relatively few reviews, mixed quality, or sparse ad presence — all signs worth paying attention to.

Look at pricing patterns as a signal

In a niche that has been heavily competed for a long time, prices tend to compress. Sellers undercut each other until margins are thin. In younger or less-competed niches, pricing is often more varied, which usually means no dominant player has set a floor yet. Looking at the range of prices on a results page — not an estimate of revenue, just the actual listed prices — can help you gauge maturity and margin potential.

Check how listings are differentiated

Open several listings in a niche you are considering. Are they essentially the same product with different thumbnail colours? Or are there genuinely different approaches, formats, or audiences being served? Sameness suggests a niche that has been copied to death. Variation — including gaps in what is not being offered — suggests there may still be room to position clearly.

Pay attention to recency signals

Etsy shows you when listings were last updated and sometimes how recently reviews were left. A niche where the top listings have reviews from two or three years ago but nothing recent can mean one of two things: the niche has died, or nobody has tried it lately. That distinction matters, and you can only work it out by looking more carefully at the specific products and whether the underlying demand is likely still there.

The Problem With Estimated Sales Data

Several popular Etsy research tools — EverBee, eRank, Alura, and others — show estimated monthly sales and revenue figures alongside Etsy listings. These tools do useful things, and many sellers rely on them. But it is worth understanding what those estimates are: Etsy does not publish sales data, so the figures shown are modelled approximations, not verified numbers. The methodology behind them varies, and the accuracy is not something you or the tool provider can independently confirm.

That does not make those tools worthless. It does mean you should treat their sales estimates as rough directional signals rather than facts. Basing a product decision on a specific estimated revenue figure carries real risk if that figure is materially off.

The alternative — or the complement — is to look only at what is genuinely visible on the page: prices, review counts, ad density, listing age, and the shape of competition. These things you can verify yourself. They are not complete, but they are real.

Digital Niches Worth Investigating (Without Making Guarantees)

Rather than listing 'hot niches' — which would be outdated almost immediately and would require sales data we do not have — it is more useful to point at types of digital products where niches tend to stay more fragmented.

  • Occupation-specific templates: Generic resume templates are saturated. Templates built specifically for teachers, nurses, software engineers, or tradespeople are narrower and often less competed.
  • Regional and cultural specificity: Printables built around specific regional traditions, school systems, or cultural calendars serve smaller audiences but face less competition from the mass-market sellers who target the broadest keywords.
  • Emerging software platforms: Every time a new productivity or creative tool gains traction, demand for templates and assets follows. Getting into those searches early — when the format is established but the Etsy supply is thin — can be effective.
  • Accessibility-focused formats: Products designed with specific accessibility needs in mind (larger text, particular colour contrasts, dyslexia-friendly layouts) are underserved in most digital categories.
  • Hyper-specific occasions: 'Birthday card printable' is crowded. 'Retirement card printable for a teacher who loves gardening' is an audience of one, but the principle — stacking specifics — applies across a spectrum of useful narrowness.

None of these are guaranteed to work. They are directions worth exploring using the method described above: search, observe real data, check review counts and pricing, assess differentiation.

Before You Make the Product

The most useful time to do niche research is before you spend time creating. A few searches using real visible data — review counts, ad density, pricing — can tell you whether a niche is wide open, tightly held by established sellers, or somewhere in between. That information shapes not just whether to enter a niche, but how to price, what to include, and how to position your listing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if a digital niche on Etsy is actually low competition?

Look at the review counts of the top organic results (not the ads). If most of the first-page listings have a small number of reviews and pricing is varied rather than compressed, that is a reasonable indicator of a less-established niche. Ad density is also useful — fewer ads at the top of a search page generally suggests lower commercial competition for that term.

Are estimated Etsy sales figures from tools like EverBee or eRank accurate?

These tools produce modelled estimates because Etsy does not publish actual sales data. The figures can provide a rough directional sense, but they are approximations rather than verified numbers. Decisions based on specific revenue figures carry the risk that those figures may not reflect reality closely. Using visible data — prices, reviews, ad presence — as a cross-check is sensible.

Do digital products face more or less competition than physical products on Etsy?

It depends on the category, but digital products generally have lower barriers to entry, which means niches can fill up faster. The advantage is that testing is cheaper — you are not investing in materials or inventory. Niche specificity tends to matter more in digital, because broad categories like 'printable planner' attract a very large number of sellers.

How often should I research competition in my Etsy niche?

Regularly, not just at the start. Etsy search results shift as new sellers enter niches, as Etsy adjusts its algorithm, and as buyer behaviour changes seasonally. Checking what the first page of your key search terms looks like every month or two — who is there, how many reviews they have, what they are charging — keeps you informed without becoming a full-time job.
